Deliverable 02 · Animation

One More Scroll?

Animation transcript

Meet Alex, a university student with a busy schedule. Every day, Alex attends classes, completes assignments, and uses a smartphone to stay connected with family and friends. One afternoon, Alex goes to the library to finish an assignment. After studying for a while, Alex decides to take a short social media break. “Just five minutes,” Alex thinks. But one video leads to another. Suddenly, thirty minutes have passed! “What? Thirty minutes already?” Alex looks at the unfinished assignment and realizes there is less time to complete the work. Alex feels frustrated because a short break has taken away valuable study time. Later that night, Alex plans to sleep at eleven. But while scrolling through social media, Alex loses track of time again. It is almost midnight! Alex wonders, “Why do I keep scrolling longer than planned?” Research has found a connection between problematic smartphone use and sleep difficulties among university students. Canadian research has also examined how screen time relates to young people's well-being. However, these findings do not mean every student experiences the same problems. The next day, Alex asks three questions: “Why am I using my phone? Is this the right time? Can I stop when I planned?” Alex realizes that phones are useful for studying, communication, and entertainment, but understanding personal habits is important. Alex also understands that small changes in daily routines can make it easier to manage recreational screen time. Now, Alex takes planned breaks and puts the phone aside at bedtime. Digital balance does not mean avoiding technology. It means making thoughtful choices about how and when we use it. Understand your habits. Make intentional choices. Find your digital balance.

Descriptive rationale

My 2D animation vedio, One More Scroll? Understanding a Student’s Digital Habits, is part of my Capstone project, Healthy Habits in a Digital World: Promoting Balanced Technology Use. Its purpose is to help university students recognize their recreational screen-time habits and make more thoughtful choices. The video follows Alex, a university student who takes a short social media break while studying but continues scrolling longer than planned. Later, the same thing happens before bedtime. These everyday situations help students understand how unplanned scrolling can interrupt their routines. The story connects with research by Yin et al. (2025) on problematic smartphone use and sleep difficulties, and Brunton et al. (2026) on screen time and young people’s well-being. The video presents these findings as associations, not proof that phone use causes harm. I created the animation in Canva using animated illustrations, moving text, transitions, and my own voiceover. I used simple language, readable captions, and clear visuals to make the message accessible. The ending encourages students to understand their habits rather than stop using technology completely. This approach encourages awareness without blaming students.

Deliverable 04 · Podcast

Between the Scrolls

Podcast transcript

Hello everyone, and welcome to One More Scroll? Finding Digital Balance in Student Life. I am really happy you are here today. Before we begin, I want to ask you something. And as you listen, you can think about your own answer. There is no right or wrong response. Have you ever picked up your phone just to check one message, watch one video, or scroll through Instagram for five minutes, but then suddenly realized that twenty or thirty minutes had passed? Maybe you were planning to finish an assignment. Maybe you wanted to go to sleep early. Or maybe you were taking a short break from studying. But somehow, that short break became much longer than you expected. I think this is a situation that many university students can understand. And sometimes, the most surprising part is that we do not even notice how much time has passed. We open one application, watch a video, move to another video, read a few comments, and then continue scrolling. Before we know it, we are still holding our phones, even though we originally planned to do something else. Today, I want to talk openly with you about this everyday experience and what it means for our digital habits. This podcast is part of my capstone project, Healthy Habits in a Digital World: Promoting Balanced Technology Use. My project focuses on encouraging healthier recreational screen-time habits among students at the University of Niagara Falls Canada, using research published between 2024 and 2026. The purpose of this podcast is not to tell students to stop using their phones. It is not about saying that social media is bad or that technology is responsible for every problem in student life. Instead, I want us to think about how we use technology, why we use it, and whether our habits are helping us or sometimes getting in the way of other important activities. During this episode, we will explore recreational screen time, sleep, studying, mental well-being, and some practical ways to find a healthier balance. I will also share research findings from recent studies and explain what those findings actually mean. And most importantly, I want this conversation to feel realistic. Because as university students, we cannot simply stop using technology. We need it for our assignments, communication, work, entertainment, and many other responsibilities. So the real question is not whether we should use technology. The question is how we can use it more intentionally. Alex’s story and recreational screen time Let me begin with a simple example. I will come back to this student later in the episode, because the story shows how one small decision can change a routine. Imagine a university student named Alex. Alex has classes during the day, assignments to complete, and messages from friends and family to answer. After attending classes, Alex decides to sit in the library and work on an assignment. Everything is going well at first. The laptop is open, the assignment instructions are ready, and Alex is planning to study for the next hour. After about twenty minutes, a notification appears on the phone. It is just a message from a friend. Alex thinks, "I will check it quickly and then continue studying." After answering the message, Alex notices a social media notification. One video looks interesting, so Alex watches it. Then another video appears. And another. Suddenly, the phone becomes more interesting than the assignment. When Alex finally looks at the time, almost thirty minutes have passed. Now the assignment is still waiting. Alex feels frustrated because the original plan was to take only a short break. Does this situation sound familiar? Maybe you have experienced it while studying, or perhaps it happens when you are getting ready for bed. Maybe your experience is different, but many of us have moments when our attention moves from one activity to another without much planning. This is one reason why I chose recreational screen time as the focus of my capstone project. Recreational screen time means using digital devices mainly for entertainment or leisure. For example, scrolling through social media, watching funny videos, playing games, or browsing online content for enjoyment. But we need to understand something important. Not every minute spent looking at a screen is recreational screen time. If you are using your laptop to write an assignment, attending an online lecture, checking university emails, or communicating with your family, those activities have different purposes. This difference matters because technology is an important part of university life. For many students, especially those studying away from home, digital communication is also an important way to stay connected with loved ones. A video call with family may take an hour, but that does not automatically mean it is an unhealthy habit. In the same way, spending several hours working on an academic project is different from spending several hours automatically scrolling through entertainment content. So rather than looking only at the number of hours, we also need to think about the purpose and timing of our screen use. Why are we using our phones? What are we doing on them? Are we choosing to continue, or are we continuing without really thinking about it? These questions are central to my project. Recent research also shows that the relationship between technology use and well-being is more complicated than simply saying more screen time is always worse. For example, Ahmed and colleagues, in their 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis, examined research about social media use, mental health, and sleep. Their review identified associations between problematic social media use and difficulties related to mental health and sleep. However, the findings also varied across studies. This is an important point. An association means that two things are related in the research, but it does not automatically prove that one causes the other. For example, if a study finds that students who report problematic social media use also report sleep difficulties, that does not necessarily mean social media is the only reason for those sleep difficulties. Students may also experience stress, academic pressure, work responsibilities, or personal challenges. So we need to be careful about how we understand research findings. We should not turn complicated research into simple statements such as "phones are ruining students' lives." That would not be accurate or helpful. Instead, we can use the research to become more aware of our habits. And awareness is a good place to begin. So here is our first takeaway: instead of counting every minute of screen time, notice what you were trying to do before you opened the app, and how you felt afterward. Screen time and sleep Now, let us move from the library to another familiar place: our bedrooms. I want to talk about something many students can relate to using our phones before going to sleep. Imagine that it is eleven o'clock at night. You have finished your classes, completed some homework, and finally have a little free time. You are tired, but you decide to watch a few videos before sleeping. You tell yourself, "Just five more minutes." But after five minutes, you find another interesting video. Then you check your messages. Then you open another application. Eventually, you look at the clock, and it is almost midnight. You had planned to sleep earlier, but your bedtime has been pushed back. The next morning, you have an early class. You wake up feeling tired and wish you had gone to bed sooner. This example does not mean that everyone who uses a phone at night will have sleep problems. But it shows how recreational scrolling can sometimes take time away from other activities. Sleep is especially important during university because students often have busy schedules. There are lectures, assignments, exams, part-time jobs, and personal responsibilities. Sometimes students already have limited time for rest. When entertainment scrolling continues longer than planned, it may make those schedules more difficult to manage. A study by Yin and colleagues in 2025 examined smartphone addiction, objectively measured smartphone use, and sleep quality and duration among university students. One interesting finding was that smartphone addiction was associated with sleep outcomes, while objectively measured smartphone use did not show the same relationship. This finding is important because it reminds us that the total amount of phone use may not tell the whole story. How a person experiences their smartphone habits may matter too. For example, two students might spend a similar amount of time on their phones. One student may use the phone intentionally, finish their activities, and put it away when needed. Another student may find it difficult to stop scrolling even when they want to sleep. Their experiences are not necessarily the same. This is why I think we should pay attention to how much control we feel we have over our digital habits. Let me ask you another question. Have you ever felt tired and wanted to sleep, but continued scrolling anyway? Maybe you were waiting for one more interesting video. Maybe you wanted to see what your friends had posted. Or maybe scrolling had simply become part of your bedtime routine. If that has happened, it might be useful to reflect on the habit. You do not need to judge yourself or feel guilty. You can simply ask, "Is this how I want to spend this part of my evening?" That small question can help you become more aware of your choices. For example, you might decide to put your phone on a table away from your bed. You might turn off notifications that are not important during the night. Or you might choose a time when you want to finish recreational scrolling. These are small actions. They do not require you to stop using social media completely. And they can be adjusted depending on your schedule. For example, a student who works evening shifts may have a different bedtime routine from a student who attends early morning classes. A student who communicates with family in another time zone may also need to use their phone later in the evening. So there is no single routine that will work for every student. The goal is to find a routine that fits your responsibilities and personal needs. Screen time and studying We have talked about evenings and sleep. Now, let us return to the daytime and another important part of university life: studying. We all know that studying requires attention. Whether we are writing an essay, preparing a presentation, reviewing lecture notes, or working on a group project, we usually need some time to concentrate. But our phones are often sitting right beside us. Sometimes they are on the desk. Sometimes they are in our hands. And sometimes they are open to a social media application even while we are trying to work. Imagine you are writing an assignment. You have just started developing an idea. Then your phone lights up. You check the notification. You answer the message. You return to the assignment. A few minutes later, another notification appears. Once again, your attention moves away from your work. Even if each interruption is short, the experience can feel frustrating. You may find yourself reading the same paragraph again or trying to remember what you were planning to write. Of course, not every notification is unnecessary. Some messages may be important. A classmate might need help with a group project. Your family might need to contact you. Or you might receive an important update from the university. So the solution is not necessarily to turn off every notification. Instead, it may help to decide which notifications need your immediate attention and which ones can wait. This connects with another research area discussed in my capstone proposal: problematic smartphone use and academic achievement. Paterna and colleagues published a systematic review and meta-analysis in 2024 examining the relationship between problematic smartphone use and academic achievement. Their research identified a small negative association between problematic smartphone use and academic achievement. In simple words, the research suggests that problematic smartphone use and academic performance may be related. But this does not mean that using a smartphone automatically leads to lower grades. It also does not mean that every student who spends time on social media will struggle academically. Academic performance depends on many factors. These may include study habits, motivation, course difficulty, personal circumstances, time management, and access to support. The important lesson is that students can benefit from noticing when recreational phone use interrupts their planned study time. For example, if you are working on an assignment and repeatedly checking entertainment content, you might ask yourself whether those interruptions are helping you or making the task take longer. If you notice that your study time is being interrupted, you can try a small change. Maybe you decide to study for twenty-five minutes before checking your phone. Maybe you keep your phone on silent while completing a difficult task. Or maybe you put it inside your bag for a short period. These are options, not strict rules. Some students may prefer longer study periods. Others may need frequent breaks. The important thing is to choose a method that works for you. I also want to point out that breaks are not a bad thing. University students need rest. Taking a break after studying can help make a long day feel more manageable. The issue is not taking a break. The issue is when a planned short break becomes much longer than expected and interferes with something else we wanted to do. Think about Alex again. Alex originally planned to take a five-minute break while studying. But that break became thirty minutes of scrolling. What could Alex do differently next time? One possibility would be to decide in advance how long the break should last. Another possibility would be to choose a different break activity, such as getting water, stretching, or walking for a few minutes. Or Alex could still use social media but set a reminder to return to studying. The best option depends on what Alex finds practical. And this is something I want to emphasize throughout this podcast. Healthy digital habits are not about being perfect. They are about making choices that support our daily routines. Is All Screen Time Bad? Now, let us talk about another important question. Is all screen time bad? I think this is something we should understand clearly. Sometimes, when people hear the words “screen time,” they immediately think about students wasting time on their phones. But technology also has many positive uses. As university students, we use technology for studying, completing assignments, communicating with professors, and staying connected with friends and family. Social media can also provide entertainment, information, and opportunities to learn new things. For international students, technology can be especially important because it helps them communicate with family members living in other countries. For example, imagine two different situations. In the first situation, a student spends one hour talking to family members through a video call. After the conversation, the student feels happy and connected. In the second situation, the same student spends one hour scrolling through social media even though an assignment is waiting. Afterward, the student feels frustrated because the assignment is still incomplete. Both situations involve screen time, but the purpose and experience are different. This is why I believe digital balance is not just about counting hours. It is also about understanding why, when, and how we use technology. Research also suggests that completely restricting social media is not always the best solution. Burnell and colleagues, in their 2025 research review, examined the effects of social media restrictions on well-being. Their findings suggested that the overall benefits were generally small or negligible, although results were different across studies. This does not mean reducing social media use cannot help. It simply means that strict restrictions may not work for everyone. For university students, completely avoiding social media may not be realistic because it is part of our daily communication and entertainment. Instead, we can focus on making small changes that fit our routines. Mental Well-Being and Supportive Language Now, I also want to talk about mental well-being. University life can be exciting, but it can also be stressful. Students have assignments, exams, financial responsibilities, part-time jobs, and personal challenges. Sometimes, social media helps us relax and feel connected with others. However, problematic social media habits may also be associated with mental-health difficulties. Ahmed and colleagues, in their 2024 research review, identified associations between problematic social media use and negative mental-health outcomes. But we should remember that these findings do not prove that social media directly causes mental-health problems. Many different factors can influence how a student feels. I think it is also important that we do not judge students for enjoying social media. Spending time online does not automatically mean someone has an addiction. Instead of blaming ourselves, we can ask simple questions. How do I feel after using social media? Do I feel relaxed and entertained? Do I feel connected with others? Or do I sometimes feel frustrated because I spent more time scrolling than I planned? These questions can help us understand our habits and make better decisions. Seven Practical Habits for Better Digital Balance So, after discussing the research, let us move to the practical part of today's conversation. What can university students actually do to develop healthier recreational screen-time habits? I want to share seven simple ideas. You do not need to follow all of them at once. You can choose one or two that work for your daily routine. These are practical suggestions, not strict rules or guaranteed solutions. The first idea is to notice your habits. Before trying to change anything, pay attention to when you usually use your phone for entertainment. Maybe you scroll while waiting for class, during lunch, or before going to sleep. Maybe you open social media whenever studying becomes difficult. You do not need to record every minute. Just notice your common patterns. Once you understand when and why you scroll, you can decide whether there is anything you want to change. The second idea is to set small and realistic goals. Sometimes, we make big plans, such as deciding not to use social media for an entire week. But that may be difficult to maintain. Instead, start with something simple. For example, you could decide to finish one section of an assignment before checking Instagram. Or you could put your phone away fifteen minutes before your planned bedtime. Small goals may be easier to follow because they fit into our normal routines. The third idea is to manage unnecessary notifications. Notifications are useful because they help us receive important messages and updates. But not every notification needs an immediate response. For example, while studying, you could keep notifications from family or university applications but silence entertainment notifications. This way, you can stay connected without being distracted by every alert. The fourth idea is to make study breaks more intentional. We all need breaks, especially after studying for a long time. But sometimes, a five-minute break becomes thirty minutes of scrolling. One simple option is to decide how long your break will be before opening social media. You could also try other activities, such as stretching, drinking water, or taking a short walk. The goal is not to avoid breaks. It is to make sure that our breaks do not regularly interfere with our planned work. The fifth idea is to pay attention to bedtime habits. Think about how often we tell ourselves, “Just one more video,” before going to sleep. Sometimes, that one video becomes many more, and our planned bedtime gets delayed. If you notice this happening, you could try using a bedtime reminder or placing your phone away from your bed. Of course, students have different schedules, so there is no perfect bedtime routine for everyone. The important thing is to find a routine that supports your own needs. The sixth idea is to remember that technology can be positive. Digital balance does not mean avoiding everything online. We can still enjoy videos, communicate with friends, learn new skills, and stay connected with family. The important question is whether our digital habits fit with our responsibilities and personal goals. For example, watching videos after finishing an assignment may be a relaxing part of your evening. There is no reason to treat every enjoyable online activity as harmful. And finally, the seventh idea is to be patient with yourself. Habits do not always change immediately. Some days, you may follow your plan, while on other days, you may spend more time scrolling than expected. That does not mean you have failed. You can simply reflect on what happened and try again. Maybe your goal was too difficult, or maybe you were tired after a long day. Understanding the situation can help you make a better plan next time. Connection to My Capstone Campaign These seven ideas connect to my capstone project, Healthy Habits in a Digital World: Promoting Balanced Technology Use. My project focuses on helping university students develop healthier recreational screen-time habits. I am using different types of digital media to share this message. My social media posts create awareness, my animation explains digital habits through a student story, and my interactive dashboard presents student-related data. This podcast allows me to discuss the research and practical tips in more detail. I am also developing a habit-tracking prototype and a microsite to bring these materials together. My project uses existing research rather than collecting new data from UNF students. So, the findings do not necessarily describe the exact experiences of UNF students. Instead, I use relevant research to encourage students to understand their habits and make better choices. Returning to Alex's Story Before we finish, let us return to Alex's story. Earlier, Alex planned to study in the library, but a short break became thirty minutes of scrolling. Later, Alex also continued watching videos beyond the planned bedtime. Now imagine the next day. Alex decides to finish one part of the assignment before checking social media. Later that evening, Alex also puts the phone away before bedtime. These are small changes, but they show that Alex is becoming more aware of personal habits. Alex still enjoys social media, but now makes more intentional choices. This is the main message of my project. Healthy digital balance is not about completely avoiding technology. It is about understanding our habits and making small improvements. Closing Reflection and Listener Challenge As we reach the end of today's episode, I want to leave you with three simple questions. Why am I using my phone? When am I using it? And can I stop when I want to? Here is a small challenge. During the next twenty-four hours, notice one moment when you usually scroll. Before opening social media, ask yourself why you are using it and whether it fits your plans. Remember, technology helps us learn, communicate, and relax. The goal is not to stop using it, but to find a healthy balance. Thank you for listening to One More Scroll? Finding Digital Balance in Student Life. I hope this episode encourages you to think about your digital habits. Remember, small changes can make a difference. Until next time, stay connected, stay aware, and find your balance.
